# Music: composition, production, mixing and mastering

A handbook for writing and producing music without hearing it. It is general: it applies to any style. A named style
(`.agents/styles/`) says which choices to make for a kind of film; this file says how to make them well.

You can compose and produce competent music. You cannot hear it. So you work from three things: craft (what is known to
work), references (music the listener likes, studied with tools) and measurement (numbers about what you made). The
listener decides taste. Your job is to give them something worth judging, and to tell them exactly what to listen for.

---

## 1. What you can do

Remind yourself of the whole toolbox before you start. Most weak music comes from using a small part of it.

- **Write notes.** Melody, chords, bass and drums, with exact start, length, pitch and velocity. You can place a note
  a few milliseconds early or late on purpose.
- **Design instruments.** Synth sounds in Vial: oscillators, unison and detune, filters, envelopes, LFOs, glide, pitch
  envelopes. Samplers from sample libraries: multi-sampled instruments, layers, round robins, drum kits. Choose and
  edit samples: trim, tune, reverse, layer.
- **Arrange.** Write patterns, place them as clips, build sections, write transitions, change energy over time.
- **Mix.** Faders, pan, EQ, compression, sidechain compression, saturation, chorus and width, reverb and delay on
  shared buses, limiting.
- **Measure.** Loudness (integrated, short-term, momentary, true peak, loudness range), each track's loudness in the
  mix, energy per frequency band, stereo width per band, envelopes (attack, decay), onsets and timing, pitch, real-time
  cost.
- **Study references.** With the listener's permission: get the audio, separate it into stems (drums, bass, vocals,
  other), transcribe stems to notes, measure tempo, key, structure, spectrum, dynamics, kick and bass shapes, sidechain
  depth.
- **Render and compare.** Render versions, measure each, compare against each other and against references.

What you cannot do: hear. Never say that something "sounds great" or "sounds warm". Say what you measured, what you
intended, and what the listener should check.

## 2. Temperament

These are attitudes, not rules. They separate music that feels made with care from music that feels generated.

**The music has a job.** Before writing, know what it is for: the picture it supports, the mood, the brand or story,
the length, where it will be heard (a phone speaker, a laptop, headphones, a store). The job decides most choices.

**Restraint.** Use fewer ideas and develop them well. One good motif, said again with small changes, is better than
five motifs. Space and silence are material. A chord can sit for a long time. A section can be quiet. Let things lie
and breathe.

**Shape energy over time.** A piece is a curve of energy: low, rising, peak, release. Change the energy with density
(how many notes and layers), register (how high), rhythm (how busy), brightness (how open the filters are), loudness
and dynamics. Change one or two of these at a time, so each change is felt.

**Every element has a reason.** A sound is there because it carries the harmony, the groove, the hook, a moment in
the picture, or the space. If you cannot name its reason, remove it. Decoration without a reason makes music cheap.

**Repetition with variation.** Listeners remember what repeats. They lose interest in what repeats exactly forever.
They lose the thread when nothing repeats. Repeat a motif, a hook or a groove, and change one thing each time: the
ending, the register, a rhythm, the instrument, the harmony under it.

**Commit to a style.** A style is a set of choices about harmony, rhythm, sound and mix. Know the conventions of the
style you write in. Break a convention only on purpose.

**Sophistication is not complexity.** It comes from voicing, restraint, groove, sound quality and timing. A simple
progression, voiced well and played with feel, is sophisticated. A busy one, voiced badly, is not.

**Signs of cheap music.** Learn to see them in your own notes:
- arpeggios that run all the time;
- plain major triads with no color, moving in obvious steps;
- bells, glockenspiel and music-box sounds used as decoration;
- everything busy all the time, with no space and no dynamics;
- a mix that is muddy (low end unclear) or tinny (too much upper-mid);
- untreated presets;
- every hit of the picture "mickey-moused" with a sound;
- melodies and hooks without a clear rhythmic or expressive purpose.

**Short pieces need fewer moves.** Do not fit a whole song into 20 seconds. One idea, one or two changes of energy,
one strong moment, a clean ending.

**A working order.**
1. Know the job and the length. List the picture's strong moments with their times.
2. Choose the style, the tempo (bars that land on the cuts), the key and the mode (the mood).
3. Draw the energy map: where it is quiet, where it builds, where the breaths and the peak are.
4. Write the bed: voiced harmony, bass, drums. Check that the chord changes are audible and the low end is clean.
5. Write the foreground: the motif or hook, its development, the few accents synced to the picture.
6. Choose and shape the sounds.
7. Mix: balance, frequency allocation, sidechain, space. Measure.
8. Master. Measure. Check that it plays in real time where it will be played.
9. Hand it over with what you measured and what to listen for.

## 3. Harmony

### 3.1 Mode and color

Choose the mode for the mood.
- Major (Ionian): open, bright, often "happy". Easy to make cheerful by accident.
- Lydian (major with a raised 4th): dreamy, floating, bright.
- Mixolydian (major with a flat 7th): relaxed, bluesy, warm.
- Dorian (minor with a raised 6th): minor with lift; cool, elegant, used in jazz, funk and house.
- Aeolian (natural minor): dark, serious, emotional.
- Phrygian (minor with a flat 2nd): tense, exotic, dark.

The same chords feel different with a different bass note, register and sound. Mode is a starting point.

### 3.2 Chord color

Plain triads sound basic. Add color:
- 7ths (maj7 warm, m7 soft, dominant 7 pulls), 9ths, 11ths, 13ths;
- suspended chords (sus2, sus4) for openness and tension that wants to resolve;
- added tones (add9, 6/9);
- slash chords (a chord over a bass note that is not its root) to move the bass smoothly or keep a pedal;
- borrowed chords (from the parallel minor or major): iv minor in a major key, ♭VI and ♭VII, which sound wistful and
  cinematic;
- secondary dominants (the V of a chord other than I) for a push toward a target chord;
- altered dominants (♭9, ♯9, ♭13) for darker, jazzier tension.

### 3.3 Harmonic rhythm

How often chords change is a strong control of feel.
- Slow changes (a chord per bar or longer) are calm and confident.
- Fast changes add motion and urgency.
- A static chord with moving bass or melody is valid and often elegant.
- Uneven lengths (a chord for 3½ beats, the next for 2, the next for 2½ bars) sound human and keep a loop from feeling
  mechanical.
- In short pieces, use few chords. Two chords can carry a whole piece if the energy changes.

### 3.4 Anticipation

Change chords slightly before the beat: an eighth or a sixteenth early ("pushed"). It gives forward motion and groove.
Do the same with bass and melody notes that belong to the next chord.

### 3.5 Voicing and voice leading

How the notes of a chord are spread matters more than which chord it is.
- **Keep common tones.** When the next chord shares a note, hold it in the same voice.
- **Move the other voices by step** (a semitone or a tone) where you can. Avoid all voices jumping together.
- **Pads should bleed.** A pad that re-strikes every chord sounds like blocks. Write pad parts as lines: a note held
  while its line stays on it, only the moving voices moving, releases long enough to overlap the next chord. The
  listener should not hear a chord stop and a new one start.
- **Guide tones define the chord.** The 3rd and 7th tell the ear what the chord is. Keep them in the middle register
  (about C3–C5).
- **Spread voicings sound open.** Close voicings sound dense. In the low register, keep intervals wide: below about C3,
  use only the root, the fifth or octaves. Close intervals down there sound like mud.
- **A static upper voicing over a moving bass** is a strong device: the same few notes on top, and the harmony changes
  because the bass changes. It works only if the bass is clear (see 6).
- **The progression must be audible.** If the bass is unclear, or the voicings never change, the listener cannot tell
  that the chord changed. Check: does each change have a clear bass note, a moving guide tone, or a rhythmic
  articulation (a stab or a re-struck chord)?

### 3.6 Tension and resolution

- Resolution to the home chord is a reward. Spend it rarely.
- A loop that never lands on the home chord floats and yearns. Ending on it at the right moment is a payoff.
- The minor iv chord in a major key, then the home chord (iv–I), is a warm, emotional cadence. In minor, iv–V–i is
  strong. A deceptive move (V to vi or to ♭VI) keeps the piece open.
- Silence before a resolution makes it larger.

## 4. Melody, motif and topline

### 4.1 Motif

A motif is a short, recognisable idea: a rhythm and a contour. Rhythm makes it recognisable more than pitch does.
Develop it:
- **repeat** it;
- **sequence** it (the same shape starting on another note);
- **vary** its ending;
- **move** it to another register or instrument;
- **displace** it (start it on another beat);
- **augment or diminish** it (longer or shorter notes);
- **answer** it (call and response: a second phrase that replies, often higher or lower, often ending differently).

### 4.2 Contour

- Steps connect. Leaps surprise. Balance them: a leap is usually followed by a step back the other way.
- A climb by step builds tension; a leap at the top marks the peak.
- A falling line releases tension. It suits endings and calm sections.
- An arch (up, then down) feels complete.
- A note that circles another (the note, its neighbour, the note) searches; adding a slowly climbing note to such a
  figure makes it reach.

### 4.3 Melody and harmony

- Chord tones are stable. Tensions (9, 11, 13) color. Use both.
- Avoid notes that clash with the chord (for example a note a semitone above a chord tone held in the accompaniment),
  unless they are brief and resolve.
- Melody notes that land when the chord changes reinforce the change. Notes that arrive early pull forward.
- Over changing chords, a hook built from notes common to all the chords (often a pentatonic set) stays consonant.

### 4.4 Rhythm and space in melody

- Syncopation (notes off the beat) gives life. A melody of only on-beat notes is stiff.
- Rests are part of the phrase. A sparse phrase with space reads as calm and confident. A phrase with no rests reads as
  urgent or yearning.
- Repeated notes with slowly changing pitch make a melody you hear through rhythm.
- A steady stream that does not divide evenly into the bar (groups of three sixteenths against four beats) runs across
  barlines and feels like a run-on sentence: urgent and forward.

### 4.5 The topline as a story

Over a stable bed (harmony, bass, drums), the topline can tell the whole story. Change one dial per section: how many
notes, how many rests, the register, repetition versus motion, tension, loudness. A common arc: sparse and low → a
reply higher up → a faster, more rhythmic stream → a continuous line that reaches → calm and low again → silence while
the bed carries on.

### 4.6 Hooks

- A hook is short (often one bar), repeated many times, with small variations.
- An answer variant (same start, different ending) every second time keeps it alive.
- Syncopation and one or two leaps make it catchy.
- The hook's sound matters as much as its notes (see 8).

### 4.7 Arpeggios

Use arpeggios sparingly. When parts arpeggiate all the time, music sounds cheap and childish. An arpeggio synced to a
moment in the picture can be very effective, because it is rare and it means something. When the director's own
sketch is an arpeggio or an ostinato, it is the identity: keep it and vary it (the `musical-identity` skill).

## 5. Rhythm and groove

### 5.1 Groove is placement and dynamics

- **Swing.** Delay the off-beat sixteenths. At 120 bpm a sixteenth is 125 ms; a delay of 15–30 ms gives about 55–62%
  swing. Straight sixteenths sound mechanical in most modern styles.
- **Micro-timing.** Small, fixed offsets (2–6 ms) make parts feel played. Hats slightly late, claps slightly early or
  late, percussion looser than kick and snare. Keep offsets deterministic.
- **Velocity.** Accents on strong positions, quieter notes between. Every hit at the same velocity sounds like a
  machine.
- **Ghost notes.** Quiet snare or percussion hits between the main hits fill the groove without crowding it.

### 5.2 Patterns

- **Four on the floor** (kick on every beat): house, disco, dance pop. Driving, steady.
- **Syncopated kick** (on 1, and off-beat positions such as the "and" of 2 or of 3): hip-hop, R&B, future beats, funk.
  Bouncy and groovy.
- **Half-time** (snare on beat 3 of the bar): trap, slow future bass. Heavy, spacious.
- **Broken and shuffled** (2-step, garage, breakbeats): skipping, nervous energy.
- **Backbeat** (snare or clap on 2 and 4) is the default anchor for most pop.
- **Hats:** sixteenths with accents; open hats on off-beats; triplet rolls (sixteenth and thirty-second triplets) as
  fills and before transitions.
- **Percussion** (congas, shakers, rims) adds motion and texture. Accent it in an uneven pattern (3+3+4+3+3 sixteenths)
  rather than evenly.

### 5.3 Fills and transitions

Rolls (snare, hat, tom), reversed sounds that peak on the downbeat, risers (filtered noise opening up), drops of
elements, and a short silence before an important downbeat. A fill marks the end of a phrase; do not fill every bar.

## 6. The low end: kick and bass

The low end decides whether music feels solid and clean or muddy and weak.

- **Only kick and bass live below about 120 Hz.** Everything else is high-passed out of that range.
- **Kick and bass must not fight.** Either interlock them (bass notes between the kicks) or hit together and duck the
  bass under the kick (sidechain, see 9.4). Both are valid; choose by style.
- **A clean, thumpy kick:** a sine that falls from about 100–150 Hz to the low fundamental (40–60 Hz) in 40–100 ms,
  decaying over 0.25–0.4 s; a short click layer above about 200 Hz for definition. Tune the kick's fundamental to the
  key (its root or fifth).
- **A clean sub bass:** a sine, with a little second harmonic so it reads on small speakers. Short notes hit; long notes
  sustain and fill.
- **An 808:** a long sine note with a pitch drop at the start, glide between overlapping notes, light saturation for
  presence. It often plays on the kick hits and slides between notes.
- **Distortion on bass** is a choice. If used, keep the sub part clean and distort the upper harmonics.
- **Keep the low end mono** (below about 150 Hz).
- **Tune everything.** A kick and a bass a little out of tune with each other beat and blur.
- **Clean does not mean thin.** The band from about 160 to 320 Hz carries warmth (chord roots, bass harmonics,
  voices). Cut what is unclear there; do not remove the band.

## 7. Arrangement and form

### 7.1 Sections and energy

Typical parts: intro, build, drop or chorus, breakdown, return, outro. Keep a stable bed and change what is on top.
Add and remove layers to move energy. A section is defined by what is missing as much as by what is there.

Contrast gives each section its identity. A new register sounds like a new voice. Continuous notes after syncopated
ones sound like a new mood. Change the instrument of a line to lift a repeat.

Vary the background at the ends of phrases: a bass fill, an octave jump, a root played an eighth late after its octave
on the beat, a chromatic approach note, a run into the next section. The bed keeps its identity, and the variation
signals that something is about to change. Place these where the foreground is calm, or they are not heard.

### 7.2 Builds

A build raises energy toward a strong downbeat:
- a melody that climbs by step on long notes, with a leap at the top;
- the bass holding underneath;
- rising density: hats, then a roll that speeds up;
- a riser: filtered noise opening, or a filter on a pad opening;
- **a breath just before the downbeat:** bass and harmony out for a beat or two, only a reversed sound or a vocal left.
  The silence makes the drop land.

### 7.3 Drops and peaks

Put the drop on the strongest moment you have (a cut, an impact, a reveal). In the drop: the full low end (kick and
bass), the groove, the hook. Keep it simple and clean; the energy comes from the low end and the groove, not from
adding parts.

### 7.4 Breakdowns

Remove the kick and the bass. Let one feature speak: a melody, a texture, or the picture's own events. Keep harmony
moving slowly. Rebuild toward the return.

### 7.5 Endings

A falling line, a breath, and a final arrival: the home chord held for the first time, or a last hit. Let the tails ring
out. Do not cut a reverb short.

### 7.6 Music for picture

- Find the picture's strong moments: cuts, impacts, reveals, motion peaks. Put structural events (drops, breaths,
  arrivals) there.
- Sync a few accents to events (a note per impact, a run along a motion). Do not sync everything; it becomes cartoon
  music.
- Tempo: if the picture cuts at regular intervals, choose a tempo where bars land on the cuts.
- The music supports the picture. Leave room where the picture speaks.

## 8. Sound: choosing and designing timbres

The sound of a part sets the mood as much as its notes.

- **Warm versus bright.** Sines, triangles, filtered saws, Rhodes-type keys and voices are warm. Open saws, squares,
  bells, glockenspiels and hard plucks are bright. Bright sounds read as cheerful, commercial or childish faster than
  you expect.
- **Synth basics.**
  - Oscillators: sine and triangle soft; saw rich; square hollow; noise for air and risers.
  - Unison and detune: width and size. Too much sounds cheap and smeared.
  - Lowpass filter: the cutoff is the brightness. Most sounds are better darker than the default.
  - Envelopes: attack softens the start (20–50 ms makes a soft pluck); decay and sustain decide pluck versus pad;
    release decides how notes join.
  - Filter envelope: how plucks "speak".
  - LFOs: slow movement (cutoff, pitch vibrato after a delay). Fast wobble is a style choice.
  - Glide: slides between notes (808s, leads).
- **Pads.** No sub-octave oscillator: it puts energy where the bass lives. Slow attack, long release, filtered, wide.
- **Plucks and keys.** Soft attack, quick filter decay, a little sustain or a delay tail.
- **Voices.** A human voice (a sung note, a vowel, chops) adds texture and sophistication. Chop it to the rhythm. Respect
  a sample's range: notes outside it may be silent or sound pitched up.
- **Samples.** Before using a sample, measure it: where its onset is, where it peaks, its pitch, its length and tail, its
  energy per band. Choose a clicky kick or a boomy kick on purpose. Layer sounds that complement (a click and a sub),
  not two that share a fundamental and beat.
- **Presets** are starting points. Change the filter, the envelope and the level so the sound fits this piece.

## 9. Mixing

### 9.1 Balance first

Most of a mix is the balance of levels. Measure each track's loudness *in the mix* (solo a track by muting the others,
so sidechains still work). Rough relations for most modern styles:
- the kick and the bass carry the weight and are among the loudest elements;
- the hook or lead is clearly audible, a few dB below the full mix;
- harmony and pads sit behind, 8–12 dB below the mix;
- textures and effects are felt more than heard.

Never assume levels. Instruments differ by 10–20 dB for the same velocity: an FM electric piano's high notes are
loud, a sampled glockenspiel is quiet, a sustained 808 is far louder than its short notes. Measure after every
change of instrument or pattern. A track that measures far too quiet or loud alone may be wrong only alone: check it
in context before you change it.

Leave headroom on the way to the master (the mix's peaks a few dB under 0 dBFS before the limiter).

### 9.2 Frequency allocation

Give each element its own range and carve the rest.
- **High-pass everything that is not kick or bass:** pads and chords about 120–300 Hz, keys 150–250 Hz, voices
  150–200 Hz, reverb and delay returns 300–400 Hz.
- **The mud zone (about 150–400 Hz):** too much sounds muddy, too little sounds thin. Cut where parts overlap;
  keep some energy.
- **The harsh zone (about 2–8 kHz):** bright synths, bells, cymbals and some vocals collect here and sound tinny. Lowpass
  synths, choose darker sounds, or cut a little. Presence for a voice or a clap (around 2.5–3.5 kHz) is good in
  small amounts.
- **Air (above about 10 kHz):** a gentle high shelf here adds openness without harshness.

### 9.3 Space and width

- Put reverb and delay on shared buses, not on each track. High-pass and lowpass the returns. Shorter and darker
  reverbs keep a mix clear.
- Sync delays to the tempo (an eighth, a dotted eighth). Ping-pong delay adds width.
- Wide highs and mids, narrow lows. Width comes from unison, chorus, delay and panning. Check that the mix still works
  in mono.

### 9.4 Sidechain compression

A compressor on a track that listens to another track (usually the kick) ducks the track on each hit.
- Bass: deep (10–30 dB), recovering within about 150–250 ms. The kick hits cleanly; the bass returns between kicks.
- Pads and keys: moderate (a few dB), release 100–200 ms. This gives the "pump" of many dance styles.
- Reverb returns: light. The reverb stays out of the kick's way.
- Use peak detection, attack at or under 1 ms, release by ear for the groove (60–200 ms is common).
- A muted copy of the kick can be the trigger when the kick itself is not wanted.

### 9.5 Dynamics and color

- Compress for consistency (voices, drums), not to flatten everything. Keep the kick's transient.
- Saturate for harmonics (a bass that reads on a phone; warmth on drums). Use a little, with oversampling.
- Parallel compression thickens drums without losing punch.

### 9.6 Measure the mix

Since you cannot listen, measure:
- loudness per track and for the mix;
- energy per octave band, compared with a reference in the same style (look at the shape: the sub band against the
  80–160 Hz band, the warmth band, the harsh band, air);
- stereo width per band (lows near mono);
- peaks and clicks;
- the spectrum over time (for masking, holes, and parts that never stop).
A difference of 3 dB or more in a band against a good reference is worth a look.

## 10. Mastering

- **Chain:** a high-pass at about 25–30 Hz; gentle tonal EQ; a glue compressor (ratio 1.5–2, attack 20–30 ms,
  release around 0.2 s, a few dB of reduction); a limiter with a true-peak ceiling of −1 dBTP.
- **Loudness:** choose by where it will play. Streaming normalises to about −14 LUFS integrated. Modern pop and
  electronic masters are often −7 to −10 LUFS. Demos and films sit around −12 to −14 LUFS. Never trade punch for a
  number.
- **The master fader comes after the limiter.** Raising it above 0 dB clips.
- **Compare at matched loudness.** A louder version always seems better.
- **Check the ends.** Let tails ring; trim silence below about −96 dBFS; fade only what needs fading.
- **Small speakers.** Make sure the bass has harmonics a phone can play.

## 11. Working with references

References are creative material. Use them according to the listener's goal: study a song, transcribe it, try new
voicings, recreate or interpolate it, or use it as direction for a new piece. Do not silently replace the requested
relationship with a different task.

If the listener wants to study a song, work out the parts they ask about (chords and voicings, bass, groove, melody
and arrangement) and put them in the studio as editable notes lined up with the song. Explain the theory with those
notes in front of them. If they want a new piece, follow the relationship they asked for and make the requested
changes. When the desired result is genuinely unclear, ask before writing.

Measure:
- tempo and key; the structure as an energy map (loudness per bar);
- harmony and melody: separate stems, transcribe them to notes, read the chords per half bar and the hook's rhythm;
- the low end: the kick's pitch fall and decay, the bass's register, note lengths and ducking depth around kicks;
- the mix: energy per band and width per band; loudness and dynamics.

Delete audio you downloaded when the work is done; audio the listener brought is theirs.

## 12. Working with the listener

### 12.1 Translate their words

Listeners describe music by feel. Translate the words into properties you can change:

| They say | Usually means | Try |
|---|---|---|
| muddy, can't hear the chords | overlapping low parts; bass unclear; too much 150–400 Hz; voicings that never change | high-pass non-bass parts; clear sub bass; remove sub-octave layers; voice chords so guide tones move |
| thin, hollow | too little 160–320 Hz; everything high-passed too high | lower the high-passes; add a low fifth or root in the chords; bass harmonics |
| tinny, harsh | too much 2–8 kHz; bright synths and bells; thin low end | darker timbres; lowpass synths; cut upper mids; add air above 10 kHz instead |
| cheap, cheesy, festive | plain major triads; constant arpeggios; bells; busy everywhere | color chords; fewer, longer ideas; remove decoration; warmer sounds |
| too happy, like an advert | major mode, bright palette, bouncy rising melodies | minor or modal harmony; warmer, filtered sounds; restraint |
| sleek, elegant | restraint, extended harmony, warm tones, clean low end, groove | fewer parts, better sounds, swing, space |
| thumpy | short, clean low end; kick with a fast pitch fall; bass ducked | sine kick; short sub notes; deep sidechain |
| clean | clear separation; controlled lows and highs; no masking | EQ allocation, sidechain, darker reverbs |
| doesn't breathe | no space, no dynamics, every section full | remove layers; rests; breakdowns; a breath before peaks |
| unmelodic | no clear topline; only texture | a short motif with a clear rhythm |

### 12.2 Iterate

- Change a few things per round. Say what changed and why.
- Keep every version, so you can go back.
- Do not over-correct. "Too bright" does not mean "make it dull"; "too simple" does not mean "make it busy". Move one
  step, measure against a reference, and ask.
- Diagnose before you fix. Find the cause in the notes, the sounds or the mix (a muddy low end can be a pad's
  sub-octave oscillator, not the bass).
- The listener's taste decides. Propose, explain your reasons, and accept their judgement.
- Ask for judgement on named points: "does the kick hit?", "can you hear the chord change at the drop?", "is it too
  dark now?". "What do you think?" gets a vague answer.

### 12.3 Respect their edits

The listener may change the music while you work. Before you regenerate or overwrite anything, check for their
changes. Keep them, or ask. Never overwrite them without saying so.

## 13. Before you hand it over

- Does it do its job (the picture, the mood, the length)?
- Is there one clear idea, developed, with space around it?
- Does the energy change, and does the strongest moment land on the strongest picture moment?
- Can you tell when each chord changes? Is each bass note clear?
- Is the low end only kick and bass, mono, tuned, and not fighting?
- Is anything decorative, busy for no reason, or arpeggiating all the time?
- Mix: per-track balance measured in context; band shape compared with a reference; no harsh upper mids; lows mono.
- Master: loudness chosen for the use; true peak at or under −1 dBTP; tails intact.
- Real time: it plays without dropouts where it will be played.
- Tell the listener what you measured, what you intended, and what to check by ear.
